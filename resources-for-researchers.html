Resources for Researchers

Methodological, reporting, and ethical guidance for running online research in psychiatry.

1. Planning and designing online surveys

Internet-based surveys: relevance, methodological considerations and troubleshooting strategies
Menon V, Muraleedharan A. General Psychiatry. 2020;33(5):e100264. doi:10.1136/gpsych-2020-100264.
Why it is relevant:

A practical overview of the opportunities and methodological challenges of internet-based surveys, including recruitment, data quality and common operational problems. Particularly relevant to Indian psychiatry researchers.

How researchers can use it:

Read this before choosing an online survey design. Use it to anticipate recruitment and data-quality issues and to plan safeguards and troubleshooting.

Open resource →
The Limitations of Online Surveys
Andrade C. Indian Journal of Psychological Medicine. 2020;42(6):575-576. doi:10.1177/0253717620957496.
Why it is relevant:

Highlights key limitations of online surveys, especially the difficulty of defining the population reached and the risk of self-selection bias.

How researchers can use it:

Use it when deciding whether an online convenience sample can answer the research question. Explicitly describe the recruitment pathway, sampling limitations and limits to generalisability.

Open resource →
The Inconvenient Truth About Convenience and Purposive Samples
Andrade C. Indian Journal of Psychological Medicine. 2021;43(1):86-88.
Why it is relevant:

Explains the implications of non-probability sampling, including convenience and purposive sampling, which are common in online recruitment.

How researchers can use it:

Use it when justifying the sampling strategy and when interpreting findings. Avoid presenting a convenience sample as representative of a wider population without supporting evidence.

Open resource →
Ideal and Maximum Length for a Web Survey
Revilla M, Ochoa C. International Journal of Market Research. 2017;59(5):557-565. doi:10.2501/IJMR-2017-039.
Why it is relevant:

Examines respondents' perceptions of web-survey length and the relationship between duration, respondent experience and data collection.

How researchers can use it:

Use when planning questionnaire burden. Keep surveys as concise as the research objectives permit, pilot the estimated completion time and state the expected duration accurately.

Introduces PsyToolkit software for programming psychological experiments.

3. Reporting online and other survey studies

Improving the Quality of Web Surveys: The Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES)
Eysenbach G. Journal of Medical Internet Research. 2004;6(3):e34. doi:10.2196/jmir.6.3.e34.
Why it is relevant:

A reporting checklist specifically developed for internet-based surveys. It addresses survey design, recruitment, consent, participation metrics, questionnaire design and prevention or handling of multiple submissions.

How researchers can use it:

Use CHERRIES when designing the survey and again when writing the manuscript. It is particularly useful for transparently reporting open versus closed surveys, view/participation/completion metrics and technical features.

Open resource →
A Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS)
Sharma A, et al. Journal of General Internal Medicine. 2021;36(10):3179-3187. doi:10.1007/s11606-021-06737-1.
Why it is relevant:

A consensus-based reporting guideline for survey studies, covering both web-based and non-web-based surveys.

How researchers can use it:

Use the CROSS checklist to check completeness of the Methods and Results sections when preparing a survey manuscript. For web-specific reporting, use it alongside CHERRIES.

Open resource →

4. Ethical guidelines and legal considerations

National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants
Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR). New Delhi: ICMR; 2017.
Why it is relevant:

The principal national ethical guidance for biomedical and health research involving human participants in India. It addresses ethics review, informed consent, risk, privacy and confidentiality, vulnerable participants and research responsibilities.

How researchers can use it:

Read the sections relevant to the study before submission to an Institutional Ethics Committee. Online recruitment and electronic consent do not remove the requirement for appropriate ethics review and safeguards.

Open resource →
Indian Psychiatric Society: The Fourteen Codes of Ethics
Indian Psychiatric Society. IPS Codes of Ethics.
Why it is relevant:

The IPS Code includes an explicit provision that psychiatrists involved in research must follow ethical principles and the requirements of the relevant Research Ethics Committee/Institutional Review Board. It addresses research applications and grants, recruitment, study conduct, interpretation, presentation and publication of findings.

How researchers can use it:

Psychiatrists should consult this alongside the ICMR guidelines and their institution's ethics requirements. It is a professional code of ethics, not a replacement for detailed national research-ethics guidance or IEC approval.

Open resource →
The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023
Government of India. Act No. 22 of 2023.
Why it is relevant:

The national law concerning processing of digital personal data. It is relevant when online research collects or otherwise processes identifiable digital personal data.

How researchers can use it:

Researchers should understand the provisions relevant to notice, consent, purpose, security, data-principal rights and responsibilities of data fiduciaries. Determine applicability and current commencement provisions for the particular research activity.

Open resource →
The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025
Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, Government of India. Gazette notification G.S.R. 846(E), 13 November 2025.
Why it is relevant:

The Rules provide implementation details under the PDPP Act, including requirements concerning notices, consent and data handling. Different provisions have different commencement dates.

How researchers can use it:

Review the Rules and the official enforcement timeline rather than assuming every provision commenced at the same time. Researchers should seek institutional/legal guidance on applicability to their study.

Open resource →

Note: This is a legal resource, not a substitute for advice from the institution's data-protection/legal office. Check for subsequent corrigenda and commencement notifications.

6. Important note for researchers

These resources are provided for education and methodological support. They do not replace approval from the relevant Institutional Ethics Committee, institutional policies, applicable law, or the responsibility of the principal investigator. Researchers should verify the latest versions of legal and ethical requirements before starting recruitment or collecting data.

7. Practical notes

  • Keep the survey short.
  • Collect only necessary sociodemographic data.
  • Do not collect personal or identifying data.
  • Consider adding attention check questions in the survey.
  • Use PsyToolkit for better randomization.
  • Contact the site owner for details on validated Gujarati scales, or follow this link
  • For online surveys, disable multiple responses from a single device to prevent repeated responses from the same participant.
  • Predefine the statistical parameters that would be used to check the quality of online data collection.
